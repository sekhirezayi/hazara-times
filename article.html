Bamyan Heritage

The Empty Niches of Bamyan: A Valley That Still Remembers Its Buddhas

For fifteen centuries the great statues watched over the valley. Two decades after their destruction, the people of Bamyan are rewriting what heritage means.

ZH
Zahra Hussaini
Heritage Correspondent, Bamyan
• 12 min read • Updated June 2, 2026

The two niches are the first thing you see when you enter the Bamyan valley. They are carved into a long cliff of soft sandstone, and even now — empty, scorched, braced with scaffolding — they command the eye. For roughly fifteen hundred years they held the largest standing Buddhas in the world. Today they hold nothing but air and the memory of what was there, and that emptiness has become, for the people who live beneath it, a story in its own right.

Bamyan sits at around 2,500 metres in the central highlands of Afghanistan, in the heart of the region historically known as Hazarajat. The valley was once a vital waystation on the Silk Road, where caravans crossing between the Iranian world, Central Asia and the Indian subcontinent stopped to rest, trade and worship. It was in this cosmopolitan crossroads, sometime between the late fifth and early seventh centuries, that monks and craftsmen carved the colossal figures into the rock.

A monument built for the road

The larger of the two statues stood roughly 55 metres tall; the smaller, some 38 metres. They were not freestanding sculptures but high-relief figures hewn directly from the cliff, their rough forms then finished in a layer of mud mixed with straw, coated in lime stucco and painted in bright colours. The folds of their robes were modelled with ropes set in the plaster. Caves honeycomb the cliff around them — once monastic cells and sanctuaries, many still bearing fragments of murals that blend Indian, Sasanian and Hellenistic styles.

We know how they appeared to medieval travellers because at least one wrote it down. The Chinese pilgrim Xuanzang passed through Bamyan around 630 CE and recorded a thriving Buddhist community of several thousand monks, along with the towering golden-hued statues and a third, reclining Buddha he described as enormous. That reclining figure has never been definitively found — and the search for it is one of the threads still drawing archaeologists to the valley.

"The stone Buddha figures, glittering with gold and precious ornaments, dazzled the eyes." — attributed to the pilgrim Xuanzang, 7th century

The day the valley fell silent

For centuries the statues survived conquest, including the campaigns of Genghis Khan, whose armies laid waste to the nearby citadel of Shahr-e Gholghola. They survived the rise and fall of empires and the slow erosion of the soft rock. What they did not survive was March 2001, when the Taliban, having declared the figures idols, destroyed them with dynamite, artillery and anti-aircraft fire over the course of several weeks. The demolition drew international condemnation and became, for many around the world, the moment the modern destruction of cultural heritage entered public consciousness.

For the Hazara, who are the majority population of Bamyan, the loss carried a particular weight. The statues had stood as a marker of the valley's deep, layered past — a past that predated the religious and ethnic lines drawn across it in more recent centuries. Their destruction was experienced not only as the loss of an artwork but as an assault on a shared inheritance.

An empty space that refuses to stay empty

In 2003, UNESCO inscribed the Cultural Landscape and Archaeological Remains of the Bamiyan Valley on the World Heritage List, and simultaneously on the List of World Heritage in Danger. The designation covers far more than the two famous niches: it takes in the surrounding caves and their murals, the ruined fortresses, and the valley itself as a cultural landscape shaped by centuries of human use.

Since then, the question of what to do with the niches has provoked one of the most thoughtful debates in modern heritage preservation. Some specialists argue for anastylosis — reassembling the statues using the original fragments salvaged from the rubble, supplemented with new material. Others insist the empty niches should remain exactly as they are: a deliberate, eloquent void that testifies to both the creation and the destruction more powerfully than any reconstruction could.

In 2015, a Chinese couple used 3D light projection to fill the larger niche with a luminous image of the Buddha for a single night — a gesture that drew thousands of residents out into the cold. For a few hours the figure stood again, made of nothing but light. Then it was gone, and the niche was empty once more.

Heritage as a living practice

What is most striking about Bamyan today is that its heritage is not confined to the cliff. The valley is a working agricultural community, its terraced fields green with potatoes and wheat in summer. A short drive away lie the cobalt lakes of Band-e-Amir, designated Afghanistan's first national park in 2009, where travertine dams hold back water of an almost impossible blue. Local guides, many of them young Hazara women and men, have built a small tourism economy around these sites.

For a younger generation, heritage preservation has become an active practice rather than a matter of mourning. There are conservation trainees stabilising the cave murals, students documenting oral histories, photographers and filmmakers recording a valley that is far more than its most famous loss. The empty niches remain — but they are no longer only a wound. They have become a question the valley asks itself, and answers, every day: what do we choose to carry forward?

Stand in the valley at dusk, when the light turns the cliff to amber and the call to prayer drifts across the fields, and the two voids seem less like absences than like open doors. The Buddhas are gone. The valley that made them is still here — and it is still making meaning.
